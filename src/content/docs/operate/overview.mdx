A cluster that runs is not yet a cluster you can operate. Plan how you will see trouble, recover from it and upgrade,
before an evaluation becomes production.

## Choose a platform

| Platform | Guide | What it gives you |
|---|---|---|
| Kubernetes | [Kubernetes](/docs/deployment/kubernetes/) | the Helm chart: members with their own volumes, gateways with their own addresses, the backup, dashboards and alert rules; tested nightly under failures |
| Docker | [Docker](/docs/deployment/docker/) | a Compose stack for evaluation and small sites |
| Amazon ECS | <a href={`${DOCS}/operations/aws-ecs.md`}>aws-ecs.md ↗</a> | example CloudFormation and Terraform templates on Graviton — validated, not yet deployed by the project |
| Hosts with systemd | <a href={`${DOCS}/operations/bare-metal.md`}>bare-metal.md ↗</a> | units for members, gateways and the backup |

## Watch the right signals

Every process exposes Prometheus metrics on its management port; five Grafana dashboards and a set of alert rules come
with the project. The questions they answer:

- **Is there a leader, and is it stable?** — no leader for 30 s, frequent elections, a member down.
- **Do gateways reach the cluster, and do they shed load?** — a disconnected gateway, requests dropped because a queue
  is full.
- **Are the sinks keeping up?** — export lag against the limit, a detached sink, sink errors.
- **Is there room on disk?** — a member below its free-space limit reports not ready before the disk is full.
- **Is the backup current?** — not recording, too far behind, snapshot too old.
- **Does authentication work?** — reject ratio, an identity store that does not answer, a home server marked dead,
  server certificates about to expire.

Per-request traces for chosen users or NAS entries, and an authentication log with the reason for every decision, help
with the cases metrics cannot explain. <a href={`${DOCS}/operations/monitoring.md`}>Metrics, dashboards and alerts ↗</a>

## Practise recovery

Replication is not a backup. Practise each procedure once before you need it:

1. **Replace a member's disk** — the member seeds itself from the others.
2. **Kill the leader under load** — and look at what your NAS saw.
3. **Restore a lost quorum from the backup** — on a copy of the cluster, not the real one.

<a href={`${DOCS}/operations/backup.md`}>Backup and restore ↗</a>

## Upgrade member by member

Restart the members with the new release one at a time, waiting until each is ready and all agree on the state before
the next. A change to how records are applied takes effect only once every member runs the new release, at the same
log position on all of them; until then a rollback is possible. Gateways can be upgraded in any order.
<a href={`${DOCS}/operations/release.md`}>Upgrading a cluster ↗</a>

## Before production

- Pin the release you evaluated, and write down the topology.
- Replace every example secret: NAS shared secrets, API tokens, certificates and keys.
- Keep the management port (metrics, health, the CoA and reload APIs) on a network only operators reach; it binds to
  `127.0.0.1` unless you change it, and the APIs exist only with a token.
- Check that storage honours fsync, and keep the durability setting at its default.
- Test your own NAS devices and authentication methods, including their retry timeouts.
- Run the continuous backup and alert on its lag.
- Practise one recovery scenario and record the result.
- Decide who upgrades, who rolls back, and who is called.
